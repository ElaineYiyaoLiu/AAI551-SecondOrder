# Sources checked on 2026-11-05

## Automation and labor demand

Acemoglu, Daron, and Pascual Restrepo (2019). “Automation and New Tasks:
How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor.” Journal of Economic Perspectives
33(2), 3–30. DOI: [10.1257/jep.33.2.3](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.33.2.3).

Checked the [publisher record](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.33.2.3)
and [author-hosted full text, pp. 3–5](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Automation%20and%20New%20Tasks%20-%20How%20Technology%20Displace.pdf).
The paper distinguishes displacement, productivity and new-task channels.
This supports separating mechanisms when studying labor demand. SecondOrder
does not represent task allocation or new-task creation, so its productivity
parameter should not be described as a replication of that framework.

## Cash transfers and demand

Egger, Dennis, Johannes Haushofer, Edward Miguel, Paul Niehaus, and Michael W.
Walker. “General Equilibrium Effects of Cash Transfers: Experimental Evidence
from Kenya.” [NBER Working Paper 26600](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26600),
December 2019, revised October 2021. DOI: 10.3386/w26600.

Checked the NBER-indexed abstract and bibliographic record. It studies one-time
cash transfers in rural Kenya and reports effects on recipient consumption and
spillovers. This motivates considering demand effects, but does not establish
SecondOrder's coefficients or its annual lag. The NBER working-paper record
reports a local multiplier of 2.4; the published 2022 version reports 2.5. No
multiplier from either version is used to calibrate this project. Direct full-text
retrieval from NBER returned HTTP 403, so this is an abstract-level check.

Published-version metadata was checked against the
[Oxford record](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/publication/2342909/ora-hyrax):
Econometrica 90(6), 2603–2643 (2022), DOI 10.3982/ECTA17945.

## Implication for this project

These sources motivate mechanism checks. They do not validate the simulator's
fixed adoption path, distribution rule or policy rankings. The one-year UBI
employment lag is a consequence of using previous consumption in demand.
The current review is targeted background work; a comprehensive novelty review
has not been completed.
